# Talking points — Lumen board presentation

These are the *spoken* notes that go with `deck.html`. The deck is sparse on
purpose — the story comes from the speaker. Each section maps to one slide.

## How to use this

Read the lede sentence verbatim. Then talk in plain language for 60–90 seconds
per slide. Resist the urge to read the numbers off the screen — they're already
there. Your job is to interpret them.

---

### Slide 1 — Title

Open with confidence and a frame: "I'm going to tell you the story of last
quarter in eight charts. We'll end with a single question the board needs to
answer."

### Slide 2 — Headline

> "Lumen is growing — 12% month over month. Most companies would stop the
> presentation here. But that one number hides three different stories, and
> the next seven minutes are about those stories."

The point is to lower the room's confidence in the headline *just enough* to
earn their attention for the breakdown.

### Slide 3 — Last 30 days

Three numbers, all up. Pause. Don't celebrate yet.

> "All three of these point in the right direction. The question I want us to
> sit with is: how much of this is repeatable, and how much is a fluke?"

### Slide 4 — Channel mix

The lede: "Three channels do 70% of the work."

Key non-obvious point: **email is the cheapest channel by margin**. Most
boards expect paid_search to be the hero. Surprise them.

> "If I gave you $10,000 and told you to spend it on growth, I'd put it into
> email list-building before paid_search. Every dollar earned through email
> keeps about 70 cents of margin. Paid_search keeps about 40."

### Slide 5 — Product head and tail

This is the classic Pareto / Zipf slide. Don't dwell on it.

The actionable line: **$48,000 in dead capital.**

> "Twenty-three products. None of them shipped more than five units in two
> months. Together they hold $48,000 worth of stock. That's $48,000 we
> couldn't spend reordering the seven products that are flying off shelves."

### Slide 6 — Cohort retention

This is the hardest slide for a non-technical audience. Slow down.

> "Imagine we grouped customers by the month they first bought from us.
> Then we asked: of that group, who came back a year later? The Q1 group is
> still buying. The Q3 group, mostly, is gone."

Pause. Let it land. Then:

> "The hypothesis is that we acquired a lot of one-time buyers through paid
> social in Q3. That's not bad — but it means a dollar spent on paid social
> isn't the same as a dollar spent on email."

### Slide 7 — Funnel

This is the *most actionable* chart in the deck. Spend the most time here.

The unlock:

> "A 1-percentage-point improvement on cart-to-checkout is worth $35,000
> a month. That's at our current traffic. We don't need more visitors. We
> need fewer of them to walk away."

### Slide 8 — Forecast

Manage expectations:

> "This isn't a crystal ball. It's a model trained on 18 months of our own
> sales. It knows about weekends and holidays. It doesn't know if Apple is
> going to launch a competing product tomorrow. The shaded band is the part
> we trust — the line is our best guess inside it."

### Slide 9 — The lever

If they remember one slide, make it this one.

> "Same shoppers. Same products. Same prices. The only difference is the
> tap-tap-tap experience. Eight points of conversion difference between
> mobile and desktop is bigger than any marketing decision we'd make
> next quarter."

### Slide 10 — The ask

Be specific about what board approval unblocks. Don't leave it ambiguous.

> "I'm asking for two things today. Sign-off on a six-week mobile-checkout
> sprint. And sign-off on a clearance plan to free up the $48,000 stuck in
> dead inventory. The email investment, I'll come back with a number after
> a 30-day test."

### Slide 11 — Thank you

Don't say "any questions?" — say:

> "I'd like to spend the next 15 minutes on whatever you'd push back on
> hardest. What's the chart that didn't convince you?"

That last line invites the substantive conversation. Most decks die at the
end because the speaker invites polite questions and gets them.
